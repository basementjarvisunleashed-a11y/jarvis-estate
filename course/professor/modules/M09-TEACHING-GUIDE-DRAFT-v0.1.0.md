## The PhD-level standard in this guide

A claim in this course is doctoral-standard only when it meets all five tests, and the professor must hold every one of them, every week:

1. **Defined:** every load-bearing term is defined exactly as taught, with its source stated.
2. **Reproducible:** the method is written so another operator, on the same own devices and own targets, can repeat it and get the same measurement, or a stated reason why not.
3. **Falsifiable:** the claim states what measurement would disprove it.
4. **Audited:** the evidence pack, the gate, and any re-measurement are filed, indexed, and versioned — never overwritten, never deleted.
5. **Defended by re-measurement:** a challenged claim is answered with a new measurement, not with a louder restatement.

If a student's work fails any test, the professor marks that failure plainly, requires re-measurement, and records the gap. That is not harshness; it is the course.


## Module M9 — Brain, Voice, and Local Intelligence: what the professor must teach, and to what depth

### Learning outcomes (teach to these, mark to these)

The student, by the end of M9, can: (1) define every load-bearing term of the module exactly, with its source; (2) run the module's methods on own devices and own targets, in the controlled lab, and record them reproducibly; (3) state the limits of each method, and what measurement would disprove a claim made with it; (4) defend a conclusion from the module against a sceptical auditor, by re-measurement where challenged.

### Deep concepts — the PhD-level core

Teach the module as a system, not as a list of tools: what the system is, what it assumes, where it breaks, and how you know it broke. For every method, teach three things together — the mechanism, the measurement, and the failure mode. A student who can only name the tool has not learned the module; a student who can explain why the tool's answer should or should not be trusted, with a measurement, has.

Teach the module's place in the whole course: which earlier modules it builds on, which later modules depend on it, and which labs carry it (see the Student Course Document and the module's lab sheets). Teach the 17-stack context where it applies — REA first, then the quick tools, Ollama tools, heavy GPU work in separate environments, and the reference libraries last — and teach honestly what is built on the Acer today and what is planned only, never presenting a planned stack as built.

### Session plan (professor side)

1. **Open with the definition, quoted exactly,** and one question it answers.
2. **Demonstrate the method once,** on an own lab target, narrating each step and each check.
3. **Hand the lab to the students,** and step back — correct method, not answers.
4. **Close with the failure mode:** what broke today, what it taught, and what re-measurement it needs.

### Common errors the professor must catch

- A definition paraphrased into a different claim.
- A measurement reported without its method, its target, or its limits.
- A tool's output treated as a conclusion, without a second measurement.
- A failure hidden, smoothed over, or reported as a success.
- A claim about a device, network, or person that was never the student's own target, in the controlled lab.

### Evidence standard and gate for M9

Gate the module on its evidence packs and its examination (blank paper, M09 in the Dean Year Pack): definitions exact, method repeatable, measurements filed, failures documented, defence answered by re-measurement where challenged. No module passes on attendance, on presentation, or on assertion.

### Marking M9 (criteria only — no key, no model answer)

Apply the 40 / 25 / 15 / 20 split (evidence pack / report / narration / defence brief) to the module's own work. Moderate by re-marking a sample of papers against the same criteria, and record where moderation changed a mark and why. Feedback must name one precise strength, one precise gap, and the exact re-measurement that would close it.
